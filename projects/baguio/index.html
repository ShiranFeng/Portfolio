Baguio

Translating a mountain city into movement

OVERVIEW

Imagining a place I had never visited

How could a travel poster communicate a city I knew only through images and written accounts? For a graphic design course, I chose Baguio and researched its misty forests, mountain scenery, and winding roads. I collected visual references, built a moodboard, and drew three compositions. A critique with my professor moved me away from symmetrical landmark views and toward a figure travelling through an imagined landscape. Sweeping curves, contrasting colours, and cloud-like lettering carried that sense of motion into the final poster.

Critique changed how I used the research. The forest, clouds, and road did not need to appear as separate attractions; their rhythm could shape the whole composition. I learned to build a visual language from references rather than simply reproduce them. Because I have never visited Baguio or tested the poster with travellers, I would still want to know whether its atmosphere communicates the destination clearly.

01 / Research

From reading about Baguio to building a visual direction

In travel guides, I found descriptions of Baguio’s cool climate, pine-covered hills and mountain scenery. Accounts of Camp John Hay’s Yellow Trail added a more sensory perspective: mist covering treetops and people walking through an uneven forest landscape. I began thinking about how a traveller might experience the place through movement. I brought forest, cloud and walking references into my moodboard to explore that direction.

Photographs and descriptions of Kennon Road drew my attention to its repeated bends. I saw a rhythm in the way the road moved across the mountains, which suggested a way to lead the viewer’s eye through the poster.

02 / Concept

Turning the landscape into an experience

While gathering visual references, I was also drawn to underwater images, particularly their sense of suspension and reflections. They made me wonder whether clouds could flow through the landscape with a similar weightless quality. I explored this through white, cloud-like shapes around the figure, eventually extending the idea into the “BAGUIO” lettering.

Fluid colour patterns offered another way to connect the elements. Their continuous curves encouraged me to explore shapes that flowed into one another, using sweeping forms and a limited, contrasting palette.

Through discussion with my professor, I developed a direction that moved beyond depicting an individual landmark. I wanted the poster to suggest the experience of exploring Baguio.

03 / Sketches & critique

Choosing the more dynamic direction

I developed three sketches: a landmark entrance, a bridge viewed in perspective, and a figure moving through an abstract landscape.

During our critique, my professor found the entrance and bridge compositions too symmetrical and static for the atmosphere we wanted to express. We selected the figure-based sketch because its pose and sweeping shapes offered a stronger sense of movement.

Comparing the sketches helped me understand that choosing relevant imagery was only part of the task. The arrangement of those elements also needed to support the intended feeling.

04 / Refinement

Connecting image and lettering

I developed the selected sketch, responding to feedback on scale and text placement. The oversized purple figure and curved orange structure established the main movement, with layers of cloud-like shapes continuing it across the page.

I developed “BAGUIO” into cloud-like lettering so that the title participated in the illustration. Green connected the composition with the forest references, while purple and orange created an expressive contrast. White linked the clouds, lettering and open spaces.

05 / Outcome & reflection

From collecting references to making decisions

The final poster presents an imaginative interpretation of Baguio through walking, flowing forms and exaggerated scale.

The project taught me to distinguish between references that explain a place and references that help develop a visual language. It also showed me how critique could turn several promising ideas into a more focused composition.

Looking back, I would examine the balance between expression and destination recognition. Mist, forests and winding roads provide a meaningful starting point, but they are not unique to Baguio. I have not evaluated whether unfamiliar viewers understand that connection or read the cloud-like title easily. A future revision would explore those questions with travellers.
